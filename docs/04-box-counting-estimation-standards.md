# Box-Counting Estimation Standards: Scale Windows, Regression, and Implementation Variance

Box-counting fractal-dimension (FD) estimation is a regression procedure layered on a sampling procedure, and reported FD values are only as standardized as the choices made in both layers. A standardized pipeline must pin 5 parameters: the minimum and maximum box sizes defining the scale window, the number and spacing of scales inside that window, the regression method used to extract the slope, the image resolution floor, and the grid-placement policy that controls quantization error. This doc records what the literature reports about each, and what happens to the estimate when they are left free.

## The scale window: minimum and maximum box size

Natural objects exhibit detail only over a finite range of scales (s_L, s_U). Below s_L, the log-count/log-size ratio converges on the object's topological dimension (1 for a skeletonized line-segment set, which is the correct box-count dimension of that finite set); above s_U, a single box covers the whole object and the ratio converges to 0 (source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2016.00149/full, jev 0.82). Any box-counting implementation must therefore first locate the range where the object actually gains detail as s shrinks, and regress only there. The literature's working example: box sizes chosen as successive powers of 2 (s = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 cm) "covering the region where the plot would usually be considered linear" (source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2016.00149/full, jev 0.82). The coarse end has a hard ceiling: the well-known upper limit of the usable range arrives where box size approaches one quarter of the object size (source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2016.00149/full, jev 0.82).

On the implementation side, every algorithm must specify the set of box calibres, including minimum and maximum sizes and the increment method between them, and the choice of linear versus exponential increment "can have a profound effect on the results of a scan" (source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Box_counting, jev 0.85). What constitutes the edge of the useful information in a digital image is itself a known decision point, since the limits employed in the scan affect the data gathered (source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Box_counting, jev 0.85).

Identifying the optimal scaling region systematically, rather than by eye, is the subject of recent dedicated methodology in segment-based box-counting (source: https://www.mdpi.com/2504-3110/9/10/633, jev 0.83).

## Number of scales and regression practice

The estimator is almost always the slope of a least-squares regression on log(N) versus log(1/s) (source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2016.00149/full, jev 0.82); tools like FracLac compute DB as the slope of the logarithmic regression of ln N on ln epsilon over the grid calibre series (source: https://imagej.net/ij/plugins/fraclac/FLHelp/BoxCounting.htm, jev 0.73). Two documented failure modes attach to this practice:

1. R-squared is misleading. The independence assumption underlying regression is violated in box-counting data, so the error estimate is deflated and R-squared is inflated. An example regression fit at R-squared greater than 0.99 showed a bowed, quadratic residual pattern, and formal tests rejected statistical self-similarity for 33 of 36 plants despite that fit quality. High R-squared does not establish even apparent fractality (source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2016.00149/full, jev 0.82).
2. The slope is a function of scale. Local (finite-difference) slope estimates across 4 scale ranges differed significantly in every pairwise comparison, meaning there is no single constant slope to estimate when the object is not statistically self-similar (source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2016.00149/full, jev 0.82).

## Quantization error and grid placement

The procedure measures the number of boxes intercepted by an arbitrary grid, not the minimum number needed to cover the object, so every count carries a strictly positive, scale-dependent quantization error (QE) that violates regression assumptions and biases local slopes (source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2016.00149/full, jev 0.82). Grid placement is not a small effect: the same image takes 12 boxes with one grid position and 14 with another at identical calibre (source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Box_counting, jev 0.85; confirmed in FracLac documentation, source: https://imagej.net/ij/plugins/fraclac/FLHelp/BoxCounting.htm, jev 0.73). Reported mitigation practice is to translate (and possibly rotate) the grid and take the minimum count, either by brute-force sampling of the grid domain or by optimization; a pattern-search formulation converged on lower error more efficiently than brute force, and its history function cut 71.1% to 93.6% of box-count evaluations. Grid translation alone may be insufficient: the literature contains evidence both for and against the independent importance of grid rotation (source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2016.00149/full, jev 0.82). Scanning from multiple orientations and averaging or optimizing the data is the general recommendation (source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Box_counting, jev 0.85). Scan type also matters: fixed-grid and sliding (overlapping) scans resample the image differently and change what is counted, especially at large box sizes (source: https://imagej.net/ij/plugins/fraclac/FLHelp/BoxCounting.htm, jev 0.73).

## Resolution floor

The practical resolution requirement shows up as a flat scaling region bounded on both ends. In the PoreSpy reference implementation, the slope-versus-box-size curve is flat at approximately 1.9 between box edge lengths of 10^1 and 10^2 pixels on a Sierpinski carpet with known FD 1.8928 (box counting recovered approximately 1.8 to 1.9), and beyond box size 10^2 the finite image size makes the result diverge (source: https://porespy.org/examples/metrics/tutorials/computing_fractal_dim.html, jev 0.81). The operational standard: the image must be large enough that a multi-decade flat slope region exists between the pixel floor and the quarter-of-object-size ceiling, and the reported window must exclude both ends (source: https://porespy.org/examples/metrics/tutorials/computing_fractal_dim.html, jev 0.81; https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2016.00149/full, jev 0.82).

## Documented variance when these are left free

The cost of leaving the window free is quantified. Across 36 root-system digitizations, the range of local slope estimates spanned over 25% of the FD estimator for three quarters of the plants; for only 4 of 36 was the max-minus-min local slope spread below 7.9% of the mean FD. That within-object scale-to-scale variation exceeded between-plant and even between-species variation in FD, making the single-number estimate neither representative nor informative for those objects (source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2016.00149/full, jev 0.82). Work on more effective computation of the box-counting dimension remains active, indicating the baseline estimator is still considered improvable (source: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2590123020300128, jev 0.94). Interactive worked examples of the estimator's sensitivity exist but carry weak backing in this corpus (weak backing, source: https://observablehq.com/@mcmcclur/box-counting-dimension-examples, jev 0.37).

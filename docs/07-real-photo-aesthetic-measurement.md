# Real-Photo Aesthetic Measurement: Stimulus Pipelines and What Transfers from Synthetic Fractals

Empirical-aesthetics research on fractal-band preference was built mostly on synthetic stimuli with fully controlled fractal dimension (D), not on uncontrolled real photographs. This doc maps the stimulus preparation pipelines the classic studies actually used, asks whether measured-D preference findings were established on real images, and assesses what that means for transferring a fractal-band taste axis from synthetic patterns to real photographs. Most quantitative grounding here is weak backing or redo dig, unweighted; the field evidence is directional, not settled.

## The synthetic baseline

The canonical preference curves come from generated images, not photographs. One long-running line builds grayscale noise images in MATLAB with 1/f amplitude spectrum slopes of 0.5, 1.2, and 2.3, then derives thresholded and edge-only variants, measuring D by box counting at approximately 0.98, 1.63, and 2.02 across the low, intermediate, and high levels (redo dig, unweighted; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6123544/). A second line generates 81 abstract monochrome fractals with the mid-point displacement technique, D from 1.1 to 1.9 in 0.1 increments, and uses a 2-alternative forced choice over 57 pairs with N = 443; overall preference peaked at D 1.2, and a mid-range 1.3 to 1.5 D model showed preference consistency not mediated by gender, age, or culture, while complexity preference was strongly predicted by individual differences (redo dig, unweighted; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4877522/). A review-style synthesis reports that natural objects tend to exhibit moderate rather than extreme complexity and builds on this with computer-generated composite fractal patterns (redo dig, unweighted; https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0272494426000253). These pipelines share a design pattern: D is set by construction, luminance and contrast are controlled, and content is stripped to low-level statistics.

## The nearest real-image bridges: silhouettes and paintings

Two pipelines bring measured D onto real-world content without going all the way to full photographs. First, Hagerhall et al. measured the fractal dimension of 80 landscape silhouette outlines and found a relationship between preference and D, proposing that fractal characteristics partly explain landscape preference; the study deliberately focused on the silhouette outline, which reduces a real scene to a 1-dimensional contour where D is tractable (redo dig, unweighted; https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0272494404000076). Second, a direct synthetic-versus-art comparison sourced 1076 paintings across 7 categories, measured D and amplitude spectrum slope on grayscale versions, partitioned paintings into low (D < 1.20), intermediate (1.3 < D < 1.50), and high (D > 1.65) groups, then built triads matched on color tone, content, and luminance within each category so judgments hinged on D. Average preference peaked at intermediate D for both the synthetic noise images and the paintings, and individual preferences correlated moderately across the two image classes, with the correlation improving when D values were more closely matched (redo dig, unweighted; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6123544/). This is the strongest published transfer evidence found: the intermediate-D preference peak does appear on real-world art, but only after heavy stimulus curation.

## Full photographs: the thinnest layer

Direct photographic evidence is thin. A Journal of Vision article connects image statistics to aesthetic preference generally (weak backing, 0.32; https://jov.arvojournals.org/article.aspx?articleid=2792593), and a 2024 Scientific Reports study concludes that the perceived beauty of art is not strongly calibrated to image statistics of the kind these pipelines measure (weak backing, 0.30; https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-69689-6). Separately, landscape-aesthetic work combining subjective questionnaires with measured fractal dimension values has been reported for real scenes (redo dig, unweighted; https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/23998083261480234), and preference work on natural vegetation imagery discusses fractal complexity in visual nature scenes (redo dig, unweighted; https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666719325002225). Work on visual adaptation to natural scene statistics reports that average preference for intermediate amplitude-spectrum slope is robust but marked individual differences exist (redo dig, unweighted; https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S004269892030198X). None of these, at the strength available here, establishes a calibrated preference curve for D on unrestricted real photographs.

## What this means for transferring a fractal-band taste axis

Three transfer constraints follow from the pipelines above. First, synthetic calibration is the strongest evidence and it is weakest exactly where transfer matters most: synthetic studies control D by construction, so a taste axis calibrated there inherits the controls and loses them on real photos. Second, the two successful real-content bridges (silhouette outlines, matched painting triads) both worked by isolating D, either by reducing the image to a contour or by matching triads on content and luminance; a transfer protocol for real photographs should reproduce the matched-triad method rather than regress preference against D in the wild. Third, the negative signal is real: at least 1 study reports weak calibration between beauty judgments and image statistics (weak backing, 0.30), and individual differences in complexity preference are large and systematic even where mid-range preference is consistent (redo dig, unweighted; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4877522/). Treat any fractal-band taste axis on real photos as a hypothesis requiring matched-D validation, not as an established measurement.

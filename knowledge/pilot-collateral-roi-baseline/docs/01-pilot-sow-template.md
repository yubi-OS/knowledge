# 01. Pilot statement of work template

**Scope:** how early-stage vendors structure pilot statements of work, including scope partitioning between vendor and customer, duration, and success and exit criteria.

## What an SOW must define

A statement of work is a project-level document that defines the scope of work, deliverables, timeline, milestones, acceptance criteria, and pricing for a discrete vendor engagement, typically used alongside a master service agreement or as a standalone project contract (weight 0.25, weak backing: thevendor.ai, https://www.thevendor.ai/templates/vendor-statement-of-work-template/). DocuSign describes it as the official blueprint for an engagement, outlining tasks, timelines, and deliverables, and notes it is also called a scope of work (weight 0.26, weak backing: docusign.com, https://www.docusign.com/templates/statement-of-work-sow). Altigee places the SOW as a document agreed between vendor and client prior to software development that covers the details and expectations of the collaboration (weight 0.12, weak backing: altigee.com, https://www.altigee.com/magazine/the-ultimate-guide-to-software-development-statement-of-work-sow-templates).

The consistent structure across these sources is: scope, deliverables, timeline, acceptance criteria, pricing. A pilot SOW for an early-stage infrastructure product adds two fields the generic template lacks: which offer the pilot exercises, and which readiness gates must close before the pilot can start. Both fields should be left as blanks or bracketed variables until a partner exists, because pre-filling them asserts customer-specific facts no evidence supports.

## Success and exit criteria are agreed, not pre-filled

Converting an enterprise pilot into a paid contract depends on agreeing measurable success criteria, a named economic buyer, and a procurement path before the pilot starts (weight 0.37, weak backing: stackmatix.com, https://www.stackmatix.com/blog/enterprise-pilot-to-paid-contract). Key Services makes the same point from the buyer side: enterprise pilots that convert share success criteria, stakeholder mapping, and pricing transitions agreed up front, and the timeline separates real buyers from free evaluators (weight 0.24, weak backing: key.services, https://key.services/insights/enterprise-pilot-to-paid-conversion). The 100tasks paid-pilot survey similarly frames a pilot as testing value, earning customer commitment, and creating evidence for a larger contract (weight 0.12, weak backing: 100tasks.com, https://www.100tasks.com/blog/paid-pilot-examples-b2b-startups).

Three independent sources converge on one rule: success criteria belong in the SOW, but their values are set jointly with the partner, not asserted by the vendor. The template discipline is to carry the criterion as a structure ("N devices enrolled with zero unintended lockouts", "SLA response time met in M of N incidents") with N and M as blanks. Writing numbers into those blanks before a partner signs invents the pilot's outcome in advance.

## Duration and scale as hypotheses

Pilot duration and fleet size follow the same discipline. The SOW template should carry a suggested range with an explicit "to be set with partner" annotation, because no evidence exists yet for what a real partner will commit to. Abovea's paid-pilot guide treats scope control and procurement preparation as the structural jobs of the pilot document (weight 0.18, weak backing: abovea.tech, https://abovea.tech/insights-strategies/how-to-structure-paid-pilot-startup/). Smartsheet's pilot success criteria template is a fill-in worksheet rather than a pre-filled example, which is the correct model: the artifact organizes the blanks, it does not answer them (weight 0.62, authoritative backing: smartsheet.com, https://www.smartsheet.com/sites/default/files/2022-05/IC-Pilot-Project-Success-Criteria-11414_PDF.pdf).

## What this means for a pilot SOW template

1. Header fields (customer, offer, duration, fleet size) as bracketed variables, each annotated as "set with partner", never as committed values.
2. A scope section split three ways: what the vendor does, what the customer provides, and an explicit readiness-gate dependency paragraph stating which gates must have evidence, not just definitions, before the pilot starts.
3. Success criteria as named structures with blank parameters, defined jointly with the partner.
4. A pricing line that references the offer catalog row and labels the number a hypothesis to be confirmed, not a commitment.
5. A data and telemetry scope line disclosing exactly what the pilot's managed service requires, up front rather than implied.

Every blank in this structure is deliberate: the SOW's job is to organize the conversation with a real partner, not to simulate one.

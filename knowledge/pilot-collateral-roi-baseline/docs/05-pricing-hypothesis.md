# 05. Pricing as hypothesis, not commitment

**Scope:** presenting pilot pricing as a hypothesis to be confirmed with the partner rather than a committed number: discovery-based pricing for early-stage B2B pilots.

## Founders guess pricing, and say so

Early-stage startups commonly guess pricing: one compilation of founder interviews reports that 67 percent of founders admit guessing, while 40 percent planned to test new pricing models in the following year, and frames pricing as a living governance decision tied to customer value, discovery, and repeatable iteration rather than a one-time launch choice (weight 0.17, weak backing: nhimg.org, https://nhimg.org/articles/customer-discovery-and-pricing-strategy-for-early-stage-startups/). The percentage itself is weakly sourced (secondary aggregator), but the qualitative claim is the load-bearing one: pricing at pilot stage is an input to discovery, not an output of it.

## Paid pilots as willingness-to-pay experiments

A paid pilot is the pricing hypothesis's test instrument. Guides on structuring B2B pilots treat the paid form as generating evidence: a pilot that tests value, earns customer commitment, and creates evidence for a larger contract (weight 0.12, weak backing: 100tasks.com, https://www.100tasks.com/blog/paid-pilot-examples-b2b-startups). One practitioner source proposes a conversion-rate reading: a healthy pilot-to-annual-contract conversion rate for early-stage B2B is above 60 percent, so five paid pilots with three conversions constitutes real evidence of willingness to pay and a repeatable sales motion (weight 0.18, weak backing: startupcorners.com, https://startupcorners.com/blog/how-to-run-a-paid-pilot-to-validate-b2b-willingness-to-pay). That 60 percent figure is weakly backed and should be treated as a placeholder benchmark, not a floor; the structural point (conversion rate is the evidence metric) is the citable part.

## Structure of a pricing hypothesis line in the SOW

Pilot-pricing guidance recommends publishing the price as a benchmark range with the actual number set per engagement. One survey of paid-pilot structures describes carrying pricing benchmarks by industry alongside sample contract clause language, with case studies including a failed pilot and why it fell apart (weight 0.32, weak backing: techhubcore.com, https://techhubcore.com/paid-pilots-for-startups/). Enterprise pilot pricing advice centers on balancing demonstrated value against business economics, and notes that the balance is struck in the pilot's design, not just its price tag (weight 0.19, weak backing: getmonetizely.com, https://www.getmonetizely.com/articles/how-to-structure-enterprise-pilot-program-pricing-effective-proof-of-concept-stra). The pilot-to-paid conversion playbook adds that procurement and the pricing transition should be agreed before the pilot starts (weight 0.37, weak backing: stackmatix.com, https://www.stackmatix.com/blog/enterprise-pilot-to-paid-contract).

Synthesizing these: the SOW pricing line should contain three things and only three things:

1. The offer row it references from the offer catalog (so the pricing is anchored to a defined scope).
2. The hypothesis label: the number is the catalog's working hypothesis, to be confirmed with the partner during the pilot.
3. The commitment boundary: nothing is a committed recurring price until the pilot itself validates it.

## Pricing transitions before kickoff

Two sources independently locate pricing-transition planning before the pilot starts rather than at its end: the conversion playbook (measurable success criteria, named economic buyer, procurement path agreed up front) and the pilot-structure guide (procurement preparation as part of the pilot document). The template consequence is that the SOW carries a "path after pilot" line even when the price is a hypothesis: the partner knows the price may move, but the path from pilot pricing to a recurring agreement does not.

## What this excludes

The hypothesis discipline excludes three anti-patterns:

1. Pre-filling the SOW with a committed number that the pilot is supposed to validate. That inverts the evidence order.
2. Quoting an industry-average price as if it were benchmarked against the actual offer. Weakly-backed benchmark figures are for orientation in discovery conversations, not for insertion into collateral.
3. Free pilots framed as pricing validation. A free pilot tests interest, not willingness to pay; the conversion-rate evidence model above depends on the pilot being paid.

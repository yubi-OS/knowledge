# 02. Product concept brief structure

Scope: How a product concept brief is structured: concept, problem, target users, value proposition, principles, features, differentiator, success metrics, risks, and the MVP recommendation, with guidance on which sections carry the argument.

## What a product brief is for

A product brief is a short planning document that captures what a team is considering building, why it matters, who it serves, and what still needs to be figured out. Product briefs are most useful at the start of product planning or product discovery, before the team has committed to a solution (https://www.atlassian.com/agile/product-management/product-brief, jev weight 0.59, authoritative). Asana's template guide describes the same artifact as a clear, concise guide to the key elements of the product plan that serves as a reference, provides consistency, and can improve product results (https://asana.com/resources/product-brief-template, jev weight 0.55, authoritative).

A fuller concept document goes beyond the brief: a product concept template captures the complete vision including market context, competitive landscape, target users, value proposition, form factors, technology approach, go-to-market strategy, and monetisation model (https://theinnovationmode.com/product-concept-template, jev weight 0.27, weak backing). The distinction matters in sequencing: the concept document is the wide first pass, the brief is the compressed second pass, and the investor one-pager (doc 03) is the third compression.

## The section set that carries the argument

Cross-referencing the template sources, the recurring section set for a concept brief is:

1. Problem statement. The brief exists to prevent scope creep, confusion, and costly execution mistakes by aligning teams on the problem, audience, and desired outcome early (https://entrepreneurs.ng/product-brief/, jev weight 0.22, weak backing).
2. Target users. Who the product serves, stated narrowly enough to be falsifiable.
3. Value proposition. The clearest articulation of the benefit; frameworks and templates for writing and testing value propositions are standard tooling here (https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/write-value-proposition, jev weight 0.32, weak backing).
4. Principles and features. Product principles (the invariants the design must respect) and a numbered feature list, each feature traceable to a problem or principle.
5. Differentiator. A single sentence on why this differs from conventional alternatives in the same category.
6. Success metrics. The observable signals that would show the product is working; template guides consistently include success metrics as a first-class section rather than an afterthought (https://asana.com/resources/product-brief-template, jev weight 0.55, authoritative; https://miro.com/templates/product-brief/, jev weight 0.36, weak backing).
7. Risks. Named risks, including the risk the product itself creates.
8. MVP recommendation and next prototype. The narrowest buildable slice plus the first end-to-end workflow to prototype.

Modern brief templates extend this with hypothesis and constraints sections: a brief defines the problem, hypothesis, success metrics, and constraints before committing engineering resources (https://www.ideaplan.io/templates/product-brief-v2-template, jev weight 0.21, weak backing), and one template explicitly structures context, hypothesis, success criteria, scope, risks, and dependencies (https://www.vantageos.tech/templates/product-brief, jev weight 0.22, weak backing).

## Which sections carry the argument

In a brief written from ideation output (as opposed to from customer research), the load-bearing sections are the problem statement, the target users, and the success metrics, because those three are the falsifiable claims. The features list is the least load-bearing at this stage: it is a hypothesis about the solution, not evidence. Template authors agree the brief's job is alignment on the problem and success criteria before build (https://www.productstrategy.co/product-brief/, jev weight 0.45, weak backing), and that a brief should highlight success metrics, key risks, and assumptions for early team alignment (https://miro.com/templates/sidekicks/product-brief-writing/, jev weight 0.35, weak backing).

Two practical rules follow for a brief generated from an ideation thread:

- Label the provenance. If the brief's content came from an external ideation tool rather than from user research, say so in the document header, and mark which claims are untested. The evaluation discipline for AI-generated content applies: output may include misinformation, missing information, or fabricated information (https://libguides.northwestern.edu/ai-tools-research/evaluatingaigeneratedcontent, jev weight 0.57, authoritative).
- Keep one MVP, not five. The MVP recommendation should name the narrowest slice that tests the core value proposition, because a prototype MVP is how a team validates the product's value proposition before testing whether the product can attract, retain, and grow a profitable user base (https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mvp-definition/, jev weight 0.76, authoritative; see doc 07).

## Caveats for corpus readers

The section set above is convergent across template vendors, but the vendors are all selling templates, and only the Atlassian and Asana sources carried authoritative weight. Structure guidance from template marketing pages should be treated as weakly backed convention, not empirical findings about what makes briefs succeed.

# 06. The dBc level scale, the anti-caustic guard, fixpoint termination, and the dynamics audit

Scope: Reporting and audit instruments: the dBc level scale (20 log10 of the effect over the null's sigma) for cross-cycle comparability, the anti-caustic guard on exact 1.0000 passes, fixpoint termination when the peak delta falls below epsilon, and the dynamics audit over the append-only event log.

## Logarithmic levels

The decibel is a relative unit of measurement equal to one tenth of a bel, expressing the ratio of two values of a power or root-power quantity on a logarithmic scale (source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decibel, jev weight 0.42, weak). The DSP literature is more precise: decibels are a quantitative measure defined through several specific dB scales, with dynamic range considerations central to their use (source: https://www.dsprelated.com/freebooks/mdft/logarithms_decibels.html, jev weight 0.77). Radio reference tables give the arithmetic feel: a 3 decibel increase is a doubling of power, and a 20 decibel increase is a power increase of 100 times (source: https://www.dxing.com/radterms.htm, jev weight 0.32, weak).

The signal-to-noise ratio is the quantity the scale was built for: it compares the level of a desired signal to the level of background noise, defined as the ratio of signal power to noise power (source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal-to-noise_ratio, jev weight 0.51). Engineering references anchor the scale to a reference level, with a defined zero point near silence (source: https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/decibel-scale, jev weight 0.69, weak).

## dBc for the loop

The loop's sonometer convention reports effects as 20 log10(|delta| / sigma_null), the dBc level scale. The design intent follows directly from the log-ratio tradition: an absolute delta means nothing across cycles with different null spreads, but the ratio of effect to null sigma is comparable everywhere, and the logarithm compresses the wide dynamic range that improvement claims span. Sigma_null plays the role of the reference level that every dB scale needs. Because the number is a level, not a probability, it composes with the exclusion-only verdicts of doc 04: the level is reported beside the verdict, and the |z| > 3 boundary is a fixed cut on the level's linear counterpart.

## The anti-caustic guard

The guard is a red-flag rule: any exact 1.0000 pass is suspicious, and any perfect score must be reported together with its rank and condition. The intuition is overfitting: a measurement that lands exactly on the ideal value is more likely to indicate a leak between the measurement and the thing measured than a genuinely perfect outcome. The map requires rank and condition beside every perfect score so the auditor can see whether the perfection came from a degenerate basis (the atomicity diagnostic of doc 03 catches one cause) or from a real effect. This is an internal design rule of the capability map rather than a claim sourced from the dig, so no external citation backs it; it is recorded here as the corpus's convention.

## Fixpoint termination

The loop stops proposing when the peak delta falls below epsilon. Saturated items legitimately contribute 0: an item whose improvements have been exhausted is not a failure of the loop, it is the loop succeeding. This pairs with the cumulative-monotonicity invariant from doc 02: the cumulative sum stays monotone while increments shrink to zero, and the fixpoint rule is the honest way to declare the end state instead of manufacturing noise-level proposals to keep the increments visible.

## The dynamics audit

The append-only event log the loop already writes supports dynamical analysis of its own history, the is-this-x paper's Phi_dyn angle: f_back (the fraction of revisited states), absorbing-state analysis, and flip rate. The mathematics of absorbing chains backs the vocabulary. A state is absorbing if, once reached, the system stays in it, with self-transition probability 1, and raising the transition matrix to higher powers converges on the absorbing behavior (source: https://math.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Applied_Mathematics/Applied_Finite_Mathematics_(Sekhon_and_Bloom)/10%3A_Markov_Chains/10.04%3A_Absorbing_Markov_Chains, jev weight 0.69). A chain is absorbing when it has at least one absorbing state reachable from any state in a finite number of steps (source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absorbing_Markov_chain, jev weight 0.10, weak). The transition matrix partitions into transient and absorbing blocks (source: https://fastercapital.com/content/Absorbing-States--The-Inevitable-End--Absorbing-States-in-Markov-Chain-Analysis.html, jev weight 0.19, weak). Standard tooling computes absorbing states, steady states, and mean first passage times directly from a transition matrix (source: https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/markovchain/vignettes/an_introduction_to_markovchain_package.pdf, jev weight 0.86), and the special structure of absorbing chains is the standard first case studied (source: https://stats.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Probability_Theory/Introductory_Probability_(Grinstead_and_Snell)/11%3A_Markov_Chains/11.02%3A_Absorbing_Markov_Chains, jev weight 0.76).

Mapped onto the loop: each directive's lifecycle state (proposed, gated, executed, verified, terminal) is a chain state; the event log is the trajectory record. A high f_back means the loop keeps reopening the same items; an unexpectedly fast mass into terminal states means the fixpoint rule may be firing early; a rising flip rate means verdicts are oscillating. All three are computed from data the loop already stores, which is what makes the audit cheap.

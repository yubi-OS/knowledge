# Stability criteria for conservative elastic systems

**Scope:** how stability of a conservative elastic equilibrium is decided (energy criterion and second variation), how bifurcation instability differs from limit-point instability, what Koiter's post-buckling theory adds, the linearized eigenvalue problem as the practical test, stability switching along a branch, and degenerate cases.

## Energy criterion: the second variation

For a conservative system the total potential energy Pi determines stability. Trefftz proposed the energy criterion in 1933; Koiter later gave it a rigorous foundation for continuous elastic bodies in "The energy criterion of stability for continuous elastic bodies" (Proc. Kon. Ned. Ak. Wet. B68, 1965, listed in the references of the Koiter IUTAM chapter). The practical test: at an equilibrium point the first variation of Pi vanishes (delta Pi = 0), and stability is governed by the second variation delta2(Pi) evaluated at that point. The MIT OCW 2.080J Lecture 9 (Wierzbicki, "Stability of Elastic Structures") works the discrete Euler column explicitly: with delta2(Pi) = (K - P l) delta-theta delta-theta, the second variation is positive for 0 < P < Pc (stable), negative for P > Pc (unstable), and vanishing of delta2(Pi) identifies the point of structural instability or buckling. Equivalently delta2(Pi) = 0 is the neutral/transition case, and the same three-way sign test (positive stable, negative unstable, zero neutral) is derived for the general one-degree-of-freedom case in the same lecture. The LibreTexts rendering of the same MIT material (section 8.2 "Trefftz Condition for Stability") confirms the identical statements: the test is physical, perturb the equilibrium by an infinitesimal admissible displacement at fixed load and check the sign of the energy increment. For discrete conservative systems the same criterion is developed in the Springer article 10.1007/BF01592294.

## Bifurcation versus limit-point instability

These are two distinct modes of instability. At a bifurcation point two (or more) equilibrium branches intersect: the primary path (pure compression in the column example) loses uniqueness at Pc and the structure "has two choices", to stay straight or to bifurcate into the neighboring deformation mode (MIT OCW Lecture 9, jev primary 0.91). The canonical example is the Euler column; for the pinned-pinned elastic column the standard critical load is Pc = pi^2 EI / L^2, obtained in the same lecture from the eigenvalue condition Nc = EI times the smallest eigenvalue of the curvature-mode quotient. At a limit point, by contrast, the structure follows a single deformation path throughout its load history and reaches a stationary point of the load-displacement curve (zero tangent slope, the limit load) with no adjacent equilibrium beyond it; there is no secondary path to switch onto. The review sources (University of Iowa "Stability Criteria: A Review", jev secondary 0.47; NovaSolver nonlinear post-buckling notes, jev secondary 0.10) agree with the computational literature on the algebraic commonality: at both a limit point and a bifurcation point the determinant of the tangent stiffness matrix vanishes, and continuation methods locate either as the point of singularity while tracing the nonlinear path. The distinguishing feature is topological, not algebraic: branching at a bifurcation point, a turning point of a single path at a limit point.

## Koiter's post-buckling theory in one paragraph

Koiter's 1945 Delft thesis "Over de stabiliteit van het elastisch evenwicht" (English translation NASA TT F 10,833, 1967; also AFFDL TR 70-25, 1970, both listed in the Springer Koiter 1981 chapter references) founded initial post-buckling theory. Its central insight, confirmed across the review sources (Budiansky, "Theory of Buckling and Post-Buckling Behavior of Elastic Structures", Adv. Appl. Mech. 14, 1974, pp. 1-65, jev primary 0.83; the Koiter IUTAM chapter "Elastic Stability, Buckling and Post-Buckling Behaviour", Springer 10.1007/978-94-009-7538-5_2, jev primary 0.82; ScienceDirect, "Stability of the bifurcation point and the initial post-buckling of an elastic column with a flexible support" (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0020768320300858, jev primary 0.87)): the bifurcation load alone does not determine real failure. What matters is the initial post-buckling slope of the perfect structure, obtained by Koiter's asymptotic expansion of the equilibrium path near the bifurcation point. If that slope is negative the perfect structure is imperfection-sensitive: a small geometric imperfection lowers the maximum attainable load below the theoretical bifurcation load, and the imperfect structure snaps through at a lower load. If the post-buckling path rises (positive slope), the structure is generally imperfection-insensitive. The Koiter IUTAM abstract frames post-buckling behaviour as the "essentially nonlinear aspect" recognized much later than the stability criterion itself.

## Linearized eigenvalue problem as the practical criterion

In practice the second-variation test is executed as a linearized eigenvalue problem: at a pre-buckling equilibrium state, solve the generalized eigenvalue problem for the load factor at which the tangent stiffness becomes singular, the associated eigenvector being the buckling mode (MIT OCW Lecture 9 derives exactly this structure for the Euler column; Springer 10.1007/BF01592294 gives the discrete conservative-system theory). The consistently linearized eigenproblem is also the standard engine for tracing post-buckling paths numerically, per the review corpus.

## Stability switching along a branch

Stability can transfer between intersecting branches. In the discrete Euler column the primary path is stable below Pc and unstable above, while the secondary (post-buckling) path is stable: with the exact cosine kinematics the resisting force is monotonically increasing in the rotation angle, a behaviour the MIT lecture calls inherently stable (LibreTexts 8.2, MIT OCW Lecture 9). Along a branch, stability is decided pointwise by the sign of delta2(Pi), so the sign test re-evaluated along the traced path is what detects the switch.

## Degenerate cases

The plain sign test is conclusive only when delta2(Pi) is strictly signed. When it vanishes identically or changes character, higher-order variations are needed: Koiter's asymptotic theory classifies bifurcations as symmetric (post-buckling path independent of the sign of the buckling displacement, typically imperfection-sensitive) versus asymmetric, and multiple (coincident) critical loads require interaction analysis; the NASA survey "A Survey of Some Buckling Problems" (1966, jev primary 0.82) illustrates exactly this catalogue of symmetric and unsymmetric bifurcations on equilibrium paths.

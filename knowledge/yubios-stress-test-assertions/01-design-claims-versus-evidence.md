# 01 Design claims versus evidence

Scope: taxonomy for separating a security design claim (a goal stated in documentation) from a demonstrated property (an outcome proven by a run or an artifact), and why trust should follow only the latter.

## A claim is a statement about intent, not a fact about the system

A design claim lives in prose: a README, an architecture page, a threat model. It says the system is intended to behave a certain way. A demonstrated property is different in kind: it is the record of an executed check under stated conditions. The assurance literature draws exactly this line. The World Bank's identification-for-development guide defines a level of assurance as "the certainty with which a claim to a particular identity during authentication can be trusted" (https://id4d.worldbank.org/guide/levels-assurance-loas, jev weight 0.70, authoritative). Note the structure: the claim is the input, and the assurance level is a measured property of the process that backs the claim. A system that publishes claims without that measurement process is publishing intent only.

NIST's identity documents make the same separation operational. SP 800-63A is explicit that its requirements "detail the acceptability, validation, and verification" of enrollment processes (https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-3/sp800-63a.html, jev weight 0.85, authoritative), and the draft 800-63-4 revision marks the Identity Assurance Level requirements section as normative (https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63a/ial/, jev weight 0.91, authoritative). The takeaway for an OS project is structural, not cryptographic: an assurance framework separates the claim (this user is who they say they are, this boot is authentic) from the normative requirement (here is what must be validated) from the evidence (the validated record). A project that ships only the first of the three has an intent document, not an assurance case.

## Where the gap opens: design flaws versus assurance gaps

Two distinct failure classes live between claim and property, and they fail differently. The first is the design flaw: the stated design itself violates a security property. A 2023 study of component-based application security documents "infractions of security properties in the business tier" as a critical problem of design, not of implementation quality (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/374684425_Software_Design_Flaw_and_Security_Assurance_Gap_in_Component-based_Application_Security_Privacy, jev weight 0.68, authoritative). If the design is wrong, no amount of testing the implementation as designed will surface it; the test inherits the flaw.

The second is the assurance gap: the design is right but the evidence chain between commitment and validation is broken. An ISC2-authored piece describes a "Delivery Assurance Gap" as "the structural discontinuity between what is commercially committed and what is technically validated" (https://www.isc2.org/Insights/2026/09/a-seven-step-framework-for-overcoming-delivery-assurance-gaps, jev weight 0.47, weak backing). A vendor-neutral glossary characterizes the gap as appearing "when evidence is incomplete, validation is weak, or accountability for review is unclear" (https://nhimg.org/glossary/assurance-gap/, jev weight 0.09, weak backing). Treat these two as directional context only, since both carry sub-0.5 weights; the underlying distinction they describe, however, matches the NIST structure above.

## What converts a claim into a property

Three mechanisms recur across the sources, and they are the mechanisms an external red team will demand:

1. A stated, checkable criterion. The claim must be decomposable into assertions a machine or a tester can evaluate. The assertion-testing literature defines assertion-based testing as validating "the expected behavior of a program" with specific statements (https://testsigma.com/blog/assertion-testing/, jev weight 0.31, weak backing); the Gatling load-testing reference operationalizes the same idea as assertions that "define acceptance criteria and have your test pass or fail based on response time or request status statistics" (https://docs.gatling.io/concepts/assertions/, jev weight 0.93, authoritative). The strong source makes the shape concrete: criterion, measurement, binary verdict.
2. An execution record. The property is demonstrated only where the check ran, under the conditions the claim speaks about. A security test that returns PASS against a server that is not present "has not tested anything" (https://dev.to/mspro3210/your-security-gate-always-passes-can-it-actually-block-a-release-522n, jev weight 0.12, weak backing). The same skepticism scales up: a boot-security property proven only in an emulator has demonstrated the emulator's configuration, not the silicon's.
3. Independent re-verification. The check must be re-runnable by someone who did not author the claim. Automated property checking exists precisely for this: researchers in the infrastructure-as-code space describe work "to automatically verifying IaC-specific properties, e.g., access paths between resources" (https://programming-group.com/members/salvaneschi, jev weight 0.71, authoritative). Property checks that an outsider can re-run convert trust in the author into replication.

## The yubiOS application

yubiOS states its architecture as claims in the README: UKI-based verified boot, bootc-based immutable root, composefs integrity, FIDO2/YubiKey identity. The repo cross-check in the source analysis (GET https://api.github.com/repos/yubi-OS/yubiOS and GET https://api.github.com/repos/yubi-OS/yubiOS/contents/tests?ref=main) found the verification tooling present as scripts and workflows: tests/verify-uki-signature.sh (1,915 bytes), tests/verify-oci-attestations.sh (4,768 bytes), three reproducibility scripts under scripts/, and a 15-script tests/vm/ harness. That inventory demonstrates the mechanism list of the section above partially exists. What it does not demonstrate is the execution record: per the source analysis, every one of the 8 stress tests lacked recent run evidence in the same fetch, and the workflow history was explicitly left as a known unknown (not verified via the actions/runs endpoint).

The honest reading, in the taxonomy of this doc: yubiOS has published claims plus a partially built verification apparatus. The claims have not yet become properties, because property status requires the execution record, and that record was absent from the evidence available at analysis time.

## What an external red team takes from this

The red team does not test the claim; it tests the gap between claim and property. Concretely: pick each claim in the README, ask what artifact would exist if the claim were demonstrated, and check whether that artifact exists and can be re-run. Claims that produce no candidate artifact are the weakest claims in the repo, regardless of how confident the prose reads. The remainder of this corpus turns that method into 8 concrete assertion sets.

# 08. Migration guidance for TLS-terminating infrastructure

Scope: how to migrate a TLS-terminating tier, the origin-side gap, PQ-preferred versus PQ-only tuning, the harvest-now-decrypt-later motivation, and compliance timelines.

## Why now: the HNDL clock

Migration is motivated by a threat that predates any quantum computer. Under the harvest-now-decrypt-later model, an adversary records encrypted traffic now and decrypts it once a cryptographically relevant quantum computer exists, so any data whose confidentiality must outlast that machine's arrival is already at risk (source: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44196-026-01526-2, weight 0.88). Enterprise guidance stresses that HNDL is not a future hypothetical: a joint advisory from CISA, NSA, and NIST has warned that adversaries may already be collecting and storing encrypted data of long-term strategic value, a position shared by the UK NCSC (source: https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-pqc-harvest-now-decrypt-later-enterprise-2/, weight 0.91). This is exactly the framing Cloudflare's own deployment docs lead with (source: https://developers.cloudflare.com/ssl/post-quantum-cryptography/, weight 0.95).

## The two decision axes

TLS migration requires decisions in two distinct areas: key exchange and authentication (source: https://www.digicert.com/articles/pqc/recommendations-for-post-quantum-tls-migration, weight 0.83). Post-quantum TLS deployments today change only the key exchange, X25519MLKEM768 replaces or supplements the classical group, while certificate signatures remain classical. An IETF working-group document catalogs the characteristics applications must consider and offers best practices for quantum-ready usage profiles in TLS-based applications and supporting protocols such as DNS (source: https://www.ietf.org/archive/id/draft-ietf-uta-pqc-app-00.html, weight 0.90).

## The origin leg is the work

For anyone behind a CDN or proxy, the edge leg is usually already done and the origin leg is not. Cloudflare's origin-side PQ share was about 15% as of late 2026 against roughly 70% client-side (source: https://blog.cloudflare.com/post-quantum-visibility/, weight 0.95), and its origin documentation is the reference for enabling PQ on the Cloudflare-to-origin connection (source: https://developers.cloudflare.com/ssl/post-quantum-cryptography/pqc-to-origin/, weight 0.95). The practical recipe for a self-hosted origin is to put a reverse proxy built against OpenSSL 3.5 in front and let it negotiate the hybrid X25519-ML-KEM-768 exchange (source: https://www.cryptomathic.com/blog/quantum-ready-cryptography-with-openssl-3.5-on-rhel-9.6, weight 0.75).

Compatibility risk is real and manageable: hybrid shares are large and some servers mishandle the oversized records, which is why Go exposes GODEBUG=tlsmlkem=0 to revert its default (source: https://github.com/golang/go/issues/76677, weight 0.94). Providers respond to this with graduated modes, PQ-only, PQ-preferred, or off, and default to the fallback-tolerant flow to reduce breakage (source: https://developers.cloudflare.com/ssl/post-quantum-cryptography/pqc-to-origin/, weight 0.95, mode detail from the source corpus). The migration posture is therefore: enable PQ-preferred first, watch your negotiated-group telemetry, then tighten.

## Measure before and after

NIST's migration program instructs organizations to identify where vulnerable algorithms are used and plan replacement or update under the transition timeline in NIST IR 8547 (source: https://csrc.nist.gov/projects/post-quantum-cryptography, weight 0.96; https://www.nist.gov/pqc, weight 0.97). The NCCoE migration project frames the first step the same way: understand the use of quantum-vulnerable public-key algorithms in hardware, software, and services, and develop roadmaps that prioritize the NIST algorithms (source: https://www.nccoe.nist.gov/applied-cryptography/migration-to-pqc, weight 0.92). For TLS specifically this is now directly observable: per-zone key-exchange-group logging lets an operator report the percentage of inbound HTTPS traffic protected by post-quantum key agreement and break it down by hostname, path, user agent, or country (source: https://developers.cloudflare.com/changelog/post/2026-08-20-pqc-key-exchange-visibility/, weight 0.74).

## Sequencing

A defensible sequence for a TLS-terminating tier: inventory your terminators and their library versions; upgrade any tier below OpenSSL 3.5 or Go 1.24; enable PQ-preferred on the client-facing leg and confirm the negotiated groups; then close the origin leg, the same steps on your own origin servers, before the compliance deadlines NIST's transition work points at. The standards risk is closed, FIPS 203 is final and RFC 10024 is published, so the remaining risk is operational: versions, toggles, and the origin gap.
## What not to do

Two anti-patterns recur in migration discussions. The first is waiting for the authentication side before touching key exchange. The IETF application guidance treats the two as separate workstreams with separate readiness levels (source: https://www.ietf.org/archive/id/draft-ietf-uta-pqc-app-00.html, weight 0.90), and DigiCert's guidance makes the same split explicit (source: https://www.digicert.com/articles/pqc/recommendations-for-post-quantum-tls-migration, weight 0.83); key exchange is deployable now and should not be serialized behind token-level PQC. The second is forcing PQ-only on day one. The compatibility evidence, oversized records mishandled by buggy servers (source: https://github.com/golang/go/issues/76677, weight 0.94), is why providers default to fallback-tolerant flows; a hard PQ-only flip trades a small security gain for breakage you will debug in production.

The evidence base for acting now rather than later is the HNDL advisory picture: the joint CISA, NSA, NIST warning that adversaries may already be collecting encrypted data (source: https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-pqc-harvest-now-decrypt-later-enterprise-2/, weight 0.91), and the formal statement that the risk window opens now, not when a quantum computer exists (source: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44196-026-01526-2, weight 0.88).

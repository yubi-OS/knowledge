# 07. Measurement caveats: what the adoption numbers actually measure

Scope: how post-quantum TLS adoption percentages are produced, where they mislead, the HelloRetryRequest double-handshake effect, and the concentration-versus-adoption trap.

## Capability is not negotiation

The first caveat is definitional. A recent measurement proposal argues that post-quantum TLS measurements must separate endpoint capability, session negotiation, certificate-chain evidence, and the provenance of missing observations, because a single "supports PQ" number conflates all of them (source: https://arxiv.org/html/2605.02978, weight 0.65, marginal backing). A server may advertise a group without ever negotiating it, and a client may support the hybrid while the observed handshake stays classical. Cloudflare's Radar methodology, for example, checks whether the negotiated key exchange is a post-quantum method such as X25519MLKEM768 (source: https://radar.cloudflare.com/post-quantum, weight 0.79), which measures the negotiated outcome over Cloudflare-visible traffic, not global capability.

## The HelloRetryRequest double handshake

The hybrid's key share is large, and the protocol's fallback behavior changes what you observe. A client announces the hybrid group and sends both the hybrid key share and a classical X25519 key share; if the server does not speak the hybrid, the hybrid share was generated for nothing and the handshake continues over the classical share (source: https://www.netmeister.org/blog/tls-hybrid-kex.html, weight 0.77). Servers that do not accept the large share in the first ClientHello force a HelloRetryRequest round trip before the hybrid can be used, which doubles the handshake. The source corpus records that Cloudflare defaults to this HRR flow toward origins to reduce compatibility risk (source: https://developers.cloudflare.com/ssl/post-quantum-cryptography/pqc-to-origin/, weight 0.95). Any adoption measurement that probes once and sees an HRR can misclassify the endpoint as non-PQ, and any latency benchmark that ignores the HRR case understates the cost for mixed fleets.

## Scale and convergence in the studies

The serious numbers come from purpose-built scans. A longitudinal study established more than 2 billion TLS handshakes across 1 million domains from 11 globally distributed vantage points and observed that, despite varied policy guidance, PQ-TLS deployment converges on a small set of constructions (source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.29005, weight 0.75; https://arxiv.org/html/2607.29005v1, weight 0.77). A sector study across 32,011 domains measured negotiated TLS parameters directly, protocol versions, cipher suites, key exchange mechanisms, certificates, and split the population at 50.7% fully classical versus 49.3% with partial post-quantum readiness (source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.16473, weight 0.74; https://arxiv.org/html/2606.16473, weight 0.75). Cloudflare and Google's own real-world TLS post-quantum experiment remains the reference case for what a full-scale rollout actually looked like (source: https://blog.cloudflare.com/the-tls-post-quantum-experiment/, weight 0.92). The SoK literature catalogues why the design space narrowed the way it did (source: https://eprint.iacr.org/2023/1873, weight 0.87).

## Concentration is not adoption

Headline domain-share numbers overcount independent operators. In a March 2025 scan, Cloudflare accounted for 96% of the sites supporting post-quantum key exchange among the top 100,000 domains (source: https://cacm.acm.org/news/post-quantum-tls-finished-the-easy-half/, weight 0.89). The source corpus carries a 49.22% share of the top million domains and explicitly frames it as concentration, not adoption, noting the ACM piece behind it was only retrievable as a search snippet (HTTP 403 on direct fetch) and should be treated as secondary reporting.

## Provider-measured shares and their blind spots

Cloudflare's client share, under 3% at the start of 2024, over 60% by February 2026, about 70% by September 2026, and origin share, about 9% to 15% in the same window, are measured on Cloudflare's network (sources: https://blog.cloudflare.com/radar-origin-pq-key-transparency-aspa/, weight 0.93; https://blog.cloudflare.com/post-quantum-visibility/, weight 0.95; https://x.com/CloudflareRadar/status/2044364347183337528, weight 0.57, weak backing). They are excellent trend indicators and self-description of one network, and poor estimators of the internet at large. When quoting any single percentage in a migration plan, name who measured it, on what traffic, and whether it is capability, negotiation, or concentration.
## A checklist for quoting any PQ-TLS number

The caveats above reduce to five questions to ask of any post-quantum adoption figure before it enters a plan. Who measured it: a provider over its own traffic, an academic scan over sampled domains, or a press summary of someone else's data. What was measured: capability (the endpoint offers the group), negotiation (the handshake actually used it), or domain share (a count weighted by ownership concentration). Which leg: client-to-edge, edge-to-origin, or end to end, since the Cloudflare readings differ by a factor of about four between legs (sources: https://blog.cloudflare.com/post-quantum-visibility/, weight 0.95; https://x.com/CloudflareRadar/status/2044364347183337528, weight 0.57, weak backing). Whether the methodology accounts for HelloRetryRequest, which can hide a capable endpoint behind a double handshake (source: https://www.netmeister.org/blog/tls-hybrid-kex.html, weight 0.77). And whether the denominator is domains or traffic, since the two rank order differently once a few providers dominate (source: https://cacm.acm.org/news/post-quantum-tls-finished-the-easy-half/, weight 0.89).

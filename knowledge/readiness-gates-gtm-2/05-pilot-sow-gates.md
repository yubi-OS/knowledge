# 05: Pilot SOW Gates

Scope: the gate that must clear before a paid pilot statement of work is signed: what a pilot agreement is, how design-partner relationships differ, and how to scope a pilot to what is actually evidenced.

## What a pilot agreement actually is

A pilot agreement is a short-term contract that allows a prospective customer to test a product or service before committing to a longer-term deal; it exists to help both parties manage risk and gather data for future decisions, and a successful pilot may lead to a full commercial agreement. Standard pilot agreements are built from a Cover Page plus hosted Standard Terms incorporated by reference, with the commercial specifics (products, fees, term, evaluation criteria) isolated in the Cover Page so negotiations stay fast (source: https://commonpaper.com/standards/pilot-agreement/, weight 0.62, strong).

The SOW or Cover Page is where scope discipline lives. A pilot SOW should name: the exact platforms and configurations being tested, the term, the fee, the success criteria that decide whether the pilot succeeded, and what the vendor may claim afterward. A SaaS statement-of-work template intended for in-house counsel stresses completing all bracketed placeholders before execution and explicitly not treating the SOW as a substitute for the main agreement (source: https://www.arca.inc/templates/saas-statement-of-work-template.docx, weight 0.30, weak backing).

## Design partners are not pilot customers

Design partners are a distinct relationship: the first users of a startup's product, working closely with the startup to provide critical feedback in exchange for early access and favorable or free pricing. The design-partner agreement exists because, unlike a non-binding letter of intent or a handshake, a contract protects both sides; standard terms include feedback obligations, deployment commitments, case-study rights, fees, and future discounts, plus IP protections so early co-development does not create due-diligence roadblocks later (source: https://commonpaper.com/standards/design-partner-agreement/, weight 0.16, weak backing).

Practitioner guidance is blunt about the difference: design partners have a contractual seat at the design table and should not be a euphemism for free pilot customers; the relationship needs real reciprocal value, and a healthy program runs 5 to 15 partners, with fewer than 5 giving insufficient signal (source: https://www.koji.so/docs/design-partner-program, weight 0.35, weak backing). A readiness ladder treats design-partner recruitment as its own evidence line because design partners are recruited for co-development while pilot customers are signed against evidenced capability; conflating them means the pilot SOW inherits expectations (roadmap influence, free pricing) the vendor never intended to grant.

## Structuring the paid pilot

Guidance on structuring paid pilots for B2B startups converges on a few preconditions before the SOW is proposed: an internal sponsor on the buyer side who can keep the work moving, a funded approval route that successful results can flow into, and a scope small enough that the pilot's outcomes are attributable to the product rather than to services work around it (source: https://abovea.tech/insights-strategies/how-to-structure-paid-pilot-startup/, weight 0.25, weak backing). Pilot-governance workflow material adds exit planning as a first-class part of the pilot design: what happens on success, on failure, and on ambiguous results should be written into the plan before the pilot starts, not negotiated after (source: https://www.b2bai.directory/workflows/pilot-poc-success-governance-exit-plan, weight 0.29, weak backing).

## Scoping to evidenced claims

The distinctive rule for a security product's pilot gate is that the SOW may only cover what prior gates actually evidenced. If the pilot gate requires, say, a hardware validation run, an independent security review, and pricing validation before signature, then the signed SOW's scope is bounded by those artifacts: the platforms the hardware run exercised, the release and management plane the review covered, and the price point the proposals confirmed. Signing a pilot for production-readiness claims, or for platforms beyond the evidenced set, converts the pilot customer into the validation guinea pig and quietly widens the SOW's implied warranty beyond anything the vendor can prove.

Common Paper's pilot structure supports this mechanically: the Cover Page's Key Terms section is where the parties fix what is being sold and evaluated, which is exactly where the evidenced-scope boundary belongs (source: https://commonpaper.com/standards/pilot-agreement/, weight 0.62, strong).

## The SOW as the gate's output artifact

The pilot SOW is not just a contract; it is the gate's evidence record. A signed SOW scoped to evidenced platforms documents, in a form a third party can read, that the vendor believed it was ready to charge money on specific, bounded claims at a specific date. That artifact is what the next gate (the pilot readout, then the reference-customer decision) evaluates against. A vague SOW degrades every downstream gate, because the readout has nothing precise to be measured against.

Design rule summary:

1. Gate 1.5-style preconditions (security review, pricing validation, hardware or platform evidence) clear before signature, not after (source: https://abovea.tech/insights-strategies/how-to-structure-paid-pilot-startup/, weight 0.25, weak backing).
2. The Cover Page or SOW names platforms, term, fee, success criteria, and post-pilot claim rights (source: https://commonpaper.com/standards/pilot-agreement/, weight 0.62, strong).
3. Design-partner agreements and pilot agreements stay distinct instruments with distinct economics (source: https://commonpaper.com/standards/design-partner-agreement/, weight 0.16, weak backing).
4. Exit criteria are written into the pilot plan before it starts (source: https://www.b2bai.directory/workflows/pilot-poc-success-governance-exit-plan, weight 0.29, weak backing).

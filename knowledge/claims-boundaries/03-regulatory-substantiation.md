# 03 - Regulators and the substantiation of security claims

Scope: Regulator and standards guidance on substantiating security claims, centered on the United States Federal Trade Commission substantiation doctrine.

## The core doctrine: substantiate before you disseminate

The FTC's position is stated on its own pages. A 2023 Notice of Penalty Offenses Concerning Substantiation records that the Commission has determined certain acts or practices concerning substantiation of product claims are deceptive or unfair and violate the FTC Act [1] (jev weight 0.96). The FTC's advertising substantiation training material puts the obligation in one sentence: before disseminating an advertisement, the advertiser must substantiate all claims, express and implied, that the ad conveys to reasonable consumers [2] (jev weight 0.92, archived under the doc 08 dig queries). Two properties matter for security marketing: the duty covers implied claims, not just literal text, and the proof must exist before publication, not after a challenge.

The FTC Policy Statement Regarding Advertising Substantiation frames how the agency evaluates this: it assesses the adequacy of the substantiation an advertiser possessed before a claim was made, and decides the need for and scope of an order against a firm that lacked a reasonable basis before disseminating an advertisement [3] (jev weight 0.97, archived under the doc 08 dig queries). Security performance claims are product claims; the doctrine applies to them as it does to any other product claim.

## Enforcement mechanics and their limits

The enforcement record in this dig is thin at high weight and should be described carefully. The FTC's legal library documents hundreds of cases per year across fraud, false advertising, privacy and other consumer-protection areas [4] (jev weight 0.46, weak backing). Weakly weighted commentary on the 2023 notice observes that it cites twelve prior penalty-offense cases, of which only two fall within the last decade, and that all involved brazen conduct [5] (jev weight 0.29, weak backing). Weakly weighted law-firm analysis notes the FTC sent substantiation notices to hundreds of companies and that enforcement of those notices was expected to draw legal challenge [6] (jev weight 0.28, weak backing). The honest summary: the substantiation doctrine is long-standing and authoritative; the specific penalty mechanics remain contested in commentary.

## Sector trends

Weakly weighted tracking resources describe FTC enforcement analysis covering 2018 through 2024 [7] (jev weight 0.30, weak backing) and heightened coordinated scrutiny in specific sectors under a new task force structure [8] (jev weight 0.26, weak backing). Neither source is security-specific, so neither supports a claim that security marketing is a current enforcement priority. What the doctrine supports is the duty to substantiate, whatever the sector.

## Adjacent standards guidance

NIST material from the EO 14028 software supply chain work provides implementation guidance for secure development practices under a May 12, 2021 executive order [9] (jev weight 0.67). NIST IR 8587, joint with CISA, provides implementation guidance to federal agencies and cloud service providers on protecting identity tokens, access tokens, and assertions [10] (jev weight 0.92). These are technical requirements rather than marketing rules; their relevance to claims discipline is that they define what a compliant implementation looks like, which is exactly the evidence base a substantiated security claim would cite.

## What this means for security claims

1. The express and implied distinction reaches tone: "impenetrable" implies an absolute claim even when not written as one.
2. The reasonable-consumer framing means the audience's understanding, not the vendor's intent, sets what was claimed.
3. Substantiation has a timestamp: evidence must predate the claim [2] (jev weight 0.92).
4. No dig source shows a security-specific FTC case, so no claim of that kind is made here; the doctrine itself is the transferable part.

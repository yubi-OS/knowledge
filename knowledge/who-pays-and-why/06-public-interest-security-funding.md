# 06. Public-interest security funding

Scope: How budget-constrained public-interest organizations (schools, municipalities, newsrooms, NGOs) fund security: grants, security-focused funders, nonprofit procurement realities.

## Government grant programs

The anchor public-security funding channel in the United States is the State and Local Cybersecurity Grant Program (SLCGP), which funds eligible entities to address cybersecurity risks and threats to information systems owned or operated by, or on behalf of, state, local, tribal, and territorial governments (source: https://www.cisa.gov/cybergrants/slcgp, jev weight 0.96, authoritative backing). The program was announced by the Department of Homeland Security on September 16, 2022, as a first-of-its-kind cybersecurity grant program specifically for state, local, tribal, and territorial governments (source: https://www.cisa.gov/topics/cybersecurity-best-practices/cybergrants, jev weight 0.94, authoritative backing). DHS maintains a broader grant-funding index for eligible entities addressing cybersecurity risks (source: https://www.dhs.gov/law-enforcement-resources/resource-type/grant-funding, jev weight 0.8, authoritative backing).

The structural property of these programs is that money flows to government entities, not directly to vendors: an operating-system project selling into this segment participates as a line item inside a grantee's spending plan, not as the grant applicant. That shapes the sales motion: the vendor's job is to fit within a grantee's planned use of funds and procurement calendar.

## Nonprofit cybersecurity funding

Beyond SLTT government grants, nonprofits access cybersecurity funding through a mix of federal programs, technology-company grant and donation programs, and capacity-building channels, with 2026 guides documenting the landscape (source: https://sectec.io/nonprofit-cybersecurity-grants/, jev weight 0.14, weak backing). Nonprofit procurement differs from enterprise procurement in having less budget discretion and more funder-driven purchasing, which means the persuasive document is often the funder's eligibility rules rather than the vendor's feature list.

## Newsroom and journalist security funding

Journalism has a distinct security-support infrastructure. The International Women's Media Foundation offers holistic physical and digital safety support to women and nonbinary journalists through emergency aid, training, partnerships, coalition building, and research (source: https://www.iwmf.org/safety/, jev weight 0.73, authoritative backing), and its Newsroom Safety Across America initiative runs safety training for newsroom and freelance journalists with an intake process to build support plans (source: https://www.iwmf.org/newsroomsafety/, jev weight 0.32, weak backing). The Society of Professional Journalists' foundation runs grant programs supporting training and tools for journalists (source: https://www.spj.org/spj-foundation-grants/, jev weight 0.91, authoritative backing). Fellowships exist that explicitly include digital security as a category: at least one program funds a one-year fellowship in newsroom innovation, data journalism, digital security, or media entrepreneurship, with salary, travel, and project funding included (source: https://fieldwork.news/grants/, jev weight 0.72, authoritative backing). The Institute for Nonprofit News maintains a resources hub that includes a Press Freedom and Safety Hub alongside fundraising guidance for newsrooms (source: https://inn.org/resources/, jev weight 0.57, authoritative backing).

## What this segment's economics look like

The common thread across all 4 channels (federal/SLTT grants, nonprofit funder programs, journalism-safety funders, fellowship money) is that the funding is restricted and the security need is real but the discretionary budget is near zero. For a security product, this produces a specific value-proposition shape: the price point must fit grant-funded line items rather than enterprise contracts, and the product's argument has to work when handed to a grants officer who is not technical.

Two consequences follow. First, "cheaper" only matters if the org has any budget at all; an organization with zero discretionary security spend needs the funding mechanism to exist before any price point matters, so a vendor entering this segment without understanding the funder landscape is selling into a wall. Second, the champion inside these organizations is often a single technologist inside an otherwise non-technical org, and identifying that person requires direct discovery because such organizations vary enormously in whether they have any technical staff at all. The buyer identification problem in this segment is therefore not a sales-ops detail; it is the segment's primary unknown.

## Discipline note

Grant programs and funder rosters are dated facts: programs are announced, renewed, and retired on government and foundation calendars. Any positioning work that depends on this segment should re-verify the current program status at the source before each use, rather than treating a funding landscape documented on one date as standing.

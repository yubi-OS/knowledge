# 07. The hardware root of trust market

Scope: The hardware security key market: FIDO2 and passkeys adoption, YubiKey positioning, TPM criticism, and how consumer-priced hardware keys are positioned against enterprise PKI and TPM.

## Passkey adoption is at global scale

The FIDO Alliance reports that passkeys have reached global scale with 5 billion passkeys now in active use across consumer and workforce environments (source: https://fidoalliance.org/the-state-of-passkeys-2026-global-consumer-and-workforce-report/, jev weight 0.86, authoritative backing). Its World Passkey Day 2026 release found near-universal awareness of passkeys, with high adoption rates among consumers and enterprises (source: https://fidoalliance.org/fido-alliance-reports-accelerating-global-passkey-adoption-on-world-passkey-day-2026/, jev weight 0.87, authoritative backing). An earlier independent survey commissioned by the FIDO Alliance found 53 percent of people reported enabling passkeys on at least one of their accounts (source: https://fidoalliance.org/passkeys/, jev weight 0.88, authoritative backing).

Adoption momentum does not settle the device-bound versus synced question. One practitioner comparison reports that 33 percent of US respondents familiar with passkeys had used the more secure device-bound passkey type (source: https://www.theengineer.co.uk/media/iuyd54cq/pdf-for-web-cyber-report.pdf, jev weight 0.52, authoritative backing). Passkeys are software-based FIDO2 credentials stored on a phone, computer, or password manager that sync across devices through cloud services, while hardware keys hold the credential on the device; both use the same FIDO2 standard and provide phishing-resistant authentication (source: https://www.1kosmos.com/resources/blog/fido2-keys-vs-passkeys, jev weight 0.28, weak backing).

## The standards floor: NIST SP 800-63B-4

NIST's Digital Identity Guidelines, SP 800-63B-4, define the requirements for authentication of subjects interacting with government information systems over networks (source: https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/63/b/4/final, jev weight 0.96, authoritative backing). The authenticator guidance draws the line that matters for hardware positioning: authentication keys are considered exportable unless the authenticator generates, stores, and uses the keys in a protected hardware environment that prevents software from accessing them (source: https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63b/authenticators/, jev weight 0.95, authoritative backing). The same document treats a subscriber-controlled wallet holding multi-factor cryptographic credentials as a recognized form of strong authentication (source: https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/SpecialPublications/NIST.SP.800-63b-4.pdf, jev weight 0.84, authoritative backing). The overall SP 800-63 revision 4 covers identity proofing, authentication, and federation for users of government systems (source: https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/63/4/final, jev weight 0.95, authoritative backing; https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63.html, jev weight 0.54, authoritative backing).

For positioning, this is the authoritative wedge: a hardware-bound key is categorically different from a synced software credential under the standard's own definitions, not just stronger by degrees.

## YubiKey positioning

Yubico positions the YubiKey as the security key for strong two-factor authentication (source: https://www.yubico.com/, jev weight 0.37, weak backing). The YubiKey 5 Series supports FIDO2/WebAuthn, FIDO U2F, and PIV smart card among other protocols, which is how a single consumer-priced device serves both web authentication and enterprise PKI-adjacent use (source: https://www.yubico.com/authentication-standards/fido2/, jev weight 0.07, weak backing). FIDO2 functionality in Yubico's authenticator tooling is restricted to FIDO2-certified keys, including YubiKey 5 Series (standard, FIPS, and CSPN), YubiKey Bio Series, and Security Key Series (source: https://docs.yubico.com/software/yubikey/tools/authenticator/auth-guide/fido2.html, jev weight 0.42, weak backing).

Yubico's investor reporting describes business models that support both incremental adoption and large-scale enterprise rollouts, including YubiKey as a Service, aimed at long-term customer relationships (source: https://investors.yubico.com/en/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2021/10/yubico-ar25-eng-260407.pdf, jev weight 0.44, weak backing). Its annual Global State of Authentication survey is used as a white-paper-level market instrument (source: https://www.yubico.com/resource-categories/white-papers/, jev weight 0.39, weak backing).

## TPM and HSM: the enterprise alternatives

The contrast set for a consumer-priced hardware key is the TPM (a chip inside the machine) and the HSM (a dedicated appliance). Entrust's comparison positions HSMs as centralized key-management hardware and TPMs as per-device roots of trust (source: https://www.entrust.com/blog/2025/09/hsm-vs-tpm, jev weight 0.47, weak backing). Security-certification study material makes the operational distinction: a TPM is a localized chip providing platform integrity and disk encryption for a single device, while an HSM is a dedicated high-performance appliance for centralized key management (source: https://www.learncybersecurity.tech/courses/comptia-security-plus/general-security-concepts/tpm-and-key-management-systems, jev weight 0.44, weak backing). Comparison guides add the deployment dimension, contrasting security architecture, use cases, and operational deployment of HSM versus TPM without blurring device trust and enterprise cryptography (source: https://www.cheapsslshop.com/blog/hsm-vs-tpm-security-comparison-use-cases-and-deployment, jev weight 0.13, weak backing; https://thisvsthat.io/hardware-security-module-vs-trusted-platform-module, jev weight 0.07, weak backing; https://certsensei.io/blog/security-plus/tpm-vs-hsm-security-plus, jev weight 0.16, weak backing; https://codesigncert.com/blog/tpm-vs-hsm-comparison-guide-compliance, jev weight 0.22, weak backing).

## The positioning argument for a consumer-priced hardware root of trust

The market data supports a 3-part positioning argument:

1. Demand context: authentication is moving to phishing-resistant FIDO2 credentials at global scale, with billions of passkeys in active use (source: https://fidoalliance.org/the-state-of-passkeys-2026-global-consumer-and-workforce-report/, jev weight 0.86, authoritative backing).
2. Standards boundary: the standard itself distinguishes hardware-protected keys from exportable software keys, so "the key never leaves hardware" is a standards-backed claim, not marketing (source: https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63b/authenticators/, jev weight 0.95, authoritative backing).
3. Ownership gap: the enterprise alternatives (TPM, HSM) are either invisible to the device owner or priced for enterprises, leaving the individual owner without a root of trust they physically control. The TPM comparison material itself confirms the TPM is a per-device chip the user does not manage, which is exactly the gap a portable, owner-held hardware key fills.

The honest risk in this argument: synced passkeys are free, ubiquitous, and good enough for most consumers, so the hardware key sells only to buyers whose job requires the device-bound property or who specifically want ownership of the key material. Segment selection should therefore target users with that job, not the general passkey-adopting population.

# 08. Unvalidated hypothesis discipline

Scope: Customer discovery discipline: why segment hypotheses stay unvalidated until real customer contact, The Mom Test, lean customer development, and the failure mode of building positioning on assumptions.

## The claim and the discipline

A segment hypothesis written before any customer conversation is, by construction, unvalidated. The customer development tradition treats this as the normal starting state, not a flaw to hide: Steve Blank's customer development methodology separates learning about customers from executing on a plan, and its foundational precept is "get out of the building and talk to customers" (source: https://gonogo.team/customer-discovery/steve-blank-method, jev weight 0.45, weak backing). Blank's own writing stresses what that contact is not: customer development is not a focus group, meaning the goal is not opinions gathered in bulk but individual evidence about real behavior (source: http://steveblank.com/2009/11/30/customer-development-is-not-a-focus-group/, jev weight 0.94, authoritative backing).

The four steps of customer development (discovery, validation, creation, company building) put customer discovery first precisely so that the segment and problem assumptions are tested before they are scaled (source: https://gonogo.team/customer-discovery/steve-blank-method, jev weight 0.45, weak backing). Lean Startup popularized the same ordering: the methodology aims to help entrepreneurs validate their assumptions and build what customers actually want through continuous learning rather than static planning (source: https://fastercapital.com/content/Lean-startup--How-to-test-and-validate-your-assumptions-with-minimal-resources.html, jev weight 0.13, weak backing).

## The Mom Test rules

Rob Fitzpatrick's The Mom Test (the book runs about 120 pages) gives 3 rules for customer conversations that produce useful evidence rather than polite validation (source: https://youstartups.com/the-mom-test, jev weight 0.47, weak backing; book text: https://theforge.mcmaster.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/The-Mom-Test.pdf, jev weight 0.82, authoritative backing). The rules, as commonly summarized:

1. Talk about their life, not your idea. Asking people to evaluate a concept invites the same polite encouragement a mom gives, which is useless as evidence (source: https://theforge.mcmaster.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/The-Mom-Test.pdf, jev weight 0.82, authoritative backing; https://www.koji.so/docs/mom-test-user-interviews, jev weight 0.12, weak backing).
2. Ask about specific past behavior, not generic opinions or hypothetical future intentions. Hypothetical future questions produce intentions, and intentions are not behavior (source: https://getolune.com/startup-glossary/the-mom-test, jev weight 0.26, weak backing).
3. Treat time, reputation, and money commitments as the only reliable signal. Compliments fail as validation; commitment is the signal that counts (source: https://www.startupkit.pro/frameworks/the-mom-test, jev weight 0.22, weak backing).

Structured practice guides operationalize this: recruit 5 to 10 people who fit the target segment, ask better questions that reveal honest feedback and real market insight, and avoid confirmation bias in the synthesis (source: https://www.early.tools/experiments/the-mom-test, jev weight 0.27, weak backing). Practical question banks emphasize problem and workaround questions over solution questions (source: https://bowora.com/guides/customer-discovery-questions, jev weight 0.13, weak backing).

## Why hypotheses stay unvalidated until contact

The lean model draws the line between conviction and evidence precisely at customer behavior. The first real sign of progress often comes before the product is finished: a customer replies, signs up, or pays because the problem already feels urgent to them, not because the pitch was good (source: https://www.crv.com/content/lean-startup-business-model, jev weight 0.14, weak backing). Until something like that happens, the segment table is a belief structure. Playbook-level guidance states the stakes plainly: building a product without validation is gambling rather than business, since the leading cause of startup failure is building something nobody wants (source: https://leanpivot.ai/playbook-02-customer-discovery/, jev weight 0.42, weak backing).

Three specific failure modes follow for a security product with written segment hypotheses:

1. The mission-derived segment. When a mission statement names the intended audience, the segment table inherits the mission's confidence without the mission's evidence. Nothing about mission fit predicts willingness to pay; only contact can.
2. The champion guess. Public-interest and small-team segments contain an internal champion whose existence the vendor assumes but cannot name. If the champion does not exist, the deal path in the segment table is fiction. Blank's discipline says to find out by talking to people in the segment, not by reasoning about the segment (source: http://steveblank.com/2009/11/30/customer-development-is-not-a-focus-group/, jev weight 0.94, authoritative backing).
3. The alternative guess. Every segment hypothesis names a next-best alternative it beats. Customers actually using an alternative is the only test that the named alternative is the real one, and interviews structured per the Mom Test surface the workaround currently in place rather than the imagined one (source: https://theforge.mcmaster.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/The-Mom-Test.pdf, jev weight 0.82, authoritative backing).

## Operating rule

The honest operating rule for any pre-contact segment table: label every segment as a hypothesis, name for each the specific customer-contact evidence that would validate it (a reply, a signup, a purchase, a named champion), and re-run the table after every real conversation. A segment table that cannot name its own falsifying evidence is not a hypothesis; it is a slogan.

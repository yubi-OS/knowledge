# 01 Fractal dimension preference bands

Scope: the empirical fractal-dimension preference peak (D 1.3 to 1.5 for statistical fractals), the contested boundaries of that peak (contour class versus cluster class, exact versus statistical fractals, the Isherwood counterevidence), and why the peak is the strongest operationalizable aesthetic constant for a taste engine.

## The universal peak across image origins

The foundational result is the consistent aesthetic preference for fractals with mid-range fractal dimension, regardless of whether the image was generated by natural processes, by mathematics, or by the human hand. The "Universal aesthetic of fractals" study reports that preference peaks within the D range 1.3 to 1.5 for all three image origins, a consistent trend across three separate stimulus panels (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0097849303001547, weight 0.75; abstract page, weight 0.78). This origin-independence is what makes the band a candidate engine constant: it is not an artifact of any one stimulus family.

## Mid-range universality and its fragility

A 2016 Frontiers in Human Neuroscience study on fractal pattern preference supports the established theory that mid-range fractal patterns are a universal construct underlying preference, but it deliberately highlights the fragility of universal claims by demonstrating individual differences in preference for the interrelated concept of visual complexity (https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2016.00213/full, weight 0.87 and 0.94). The same article notes that responses to visual complexity have been reported as both linear (Forsythe et al. 2011) and curvilinear (Berlyne 1970), so the shape of the preference curve itself is contested at the edges even where the mid-range peak replicates (same URL, weight 0.87).

## What natural scenes actually span

A 2025 study of leaf and branching structures in visual nature reports that nature's fractals typically vary in complexity, measured as fractal dimension D, from 1.1 to 1.9, and that previous studies show consistent preferences across these patterns with differences in how their complexity is perceived (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666719325002225, weight 0.90). For engine design this matters: the preferred band 1.3 to 1.5 sits inside the natural range but does not cover it, so out-of-band measurements are common in real scenes and are not automatically aesthetic failures.

## A physiological story behind the band

The "fractal fluency" chapter proposes that the visual system adapts to efficiently process nature's fractal statistics, and asks whether fractal fluency creates a unique aesthetic quality because mid-range fractals are relatively easy to process, citing peaks in alpha-band EEG response for D 1.3 to 1.5 fractals as evidence of a relaxation state (https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/blogs.uoregon.edu/dist/e/12535/files/2016/02/Fractal-Fluency-Chapter-1mjdxj5.pdf, weight 0.77). The mechanism is a plausible grounding, not a proof: fluency-based accounts trace preference to processing ease, which is consistent with but does not derive the band from first principles.

## A tension from neuroscience: tuning versus preference

An independent line measures how visual cortex responds to 1/f statistics rather than what people prefer. A NeuroImage study reports that a sparse coding model predicts the most "natural" 1/f slope in its stimulus set, alpha 1.25 corresponding to D 1.59, should elicit the lowest cortical response, with responses rising outside that range to minimize metabolic cost (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1053811916305602, weight 0.93). Note that this tuning estimate (D 1.59) sits at the upper edge of, or above, the preference band. Efficiency-of-encoding and preference are related but not identical quantities, and a taste engine should not collapse them.

## Contested boundaries (honest limits)

Three boundary questions remain open, and the dig returned only indirect external support for them:

1. Contour class versus cluster class. The project record states that Spehar's contour-versus-cluster work puts contour-class peaks lower (roughly D 1.1 to 1.3) than cluster-class peaks (roughly D 1.5 to 1.7), and that a large 2016 sample observed the preference peak near D 1.2 rather than 1.3 to 1.5 (project record, source doc 2026-10-05). This dig did not surface that specific paper; the closest external hits were the 1/f tuning study above (weight 0.93) and the leaf-preference study (weight 0.90), which corroborate that peak location depends on stimulus class but do not directly confirm the D 1.2 figure. Weak external backing for the exact counterevidence; treat the band location as an open empirical question, not a settled constant.

2. Exact versus statistical fractals. The project record states the peak is contested for exact (recursively constructed) fractals, which trend toward a monotonic preference response, while it is robust for statistical fractals (project record, source doc 2026-10-05). An engine flag separating exact from statistical fractals follows from this and is cheap to implement.

3. Band location on a given measurement pipeline. The Isherwood counterevidence and the contour-class issue both reduce to the same design rule: the band must be calibrated on the engine's own measured D distribution, not assumed from the literature's scale. This is developed fully in doc 08.

## Why this axis is first in the engine

The peak is the strongest operationalizable aesthetic constant available: it has a numeric form (D), a replicable measurement method (box counting, doc 06), a cross-origin preference result (weight 0.75), and a known failure mode (pipeline dependence, doc 08). That combination is why the engine's MVP scope is one axis, fractal_band, proven on one artifact class before anything else is admitted.

# Security audit evidence standards

Scope: what a security audit gate must produce as evidence: report structure, severity thresholds, remediation plans, disclosure handling, and the public summary.

## The written report is the artifact

The gate's primary evidence is a written report, not a verbal assurance. Standard audit-report structure runs from an executive summary to detailed findings, each with risk ratings, supporting evidence, and management responses (w=0.587, high backing) [https://forensicspot.com/topics/information-security-audit-and-compliance/audit-report-structure-and-findings]. A gate that accepts an audit should require exactly that structure, because each component does a job: the executive summary supports the launch decision, the findings detail supports remediation, and the management response records who owns what.

Regulation shows the same shape at the formal end of the spectrum. Under 2 CFR 200.516, the auditor must report significant deficiencies and material weaknesses in internal control over major programs as audit findings in a schedule of findings and questioned costs (w=0.902, high backing) [https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/subtitle-A/chapter-II/part-200/subpart-F/subject-group-ECFRea73e47c9a286e6/section-200.516]. Under 32 CFR 117.8, security reporting requirements demand a written statement that indicates whether specific factors, such as deliberate disregard of security requirements or negligence in handling protected material, are evident (w=0.875, high backing) [https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-32/subtitle-A/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-117/section-117.8]. The lesson for a launch gate is structural: written reports, enumerated findings, and explicit statements about cause are the standard, and a report that cannot answer "what was found, how bad, and why" is not gate-grade evidence.

## Severity classification

Findings are classified by severity, commonly rated Critical, High, Medium, or Low, with ratings derived from the combination of likelihood of exploitation and potential impact to confidentiality, integrity, or availability (w=0.087, weak backing) [https://cyberauditauthority.com/cybersecurity-audit-findings-remediation/]. A falsifiable gate criterion then keys on the classes: zero open Critical findings and zero open High findings on the launch surface. The classification ladder is what makes "zero open Critical and High" checkable rather than rhetorical.

## Remediation plans and time-bound tracking

A Medium finding should not block a launch, but it must have a documented remediation plan. Remediation tracking converts audit findings into assigned, time-bound actions that close control gaps before the next assessment cycle, covering management action plan structure, ownership, evidence collection, follow-up verification, and escalation paths (w=0.326, weak backing) [https://forensicspot.com/topics/information-security-audit-and-compliance/remediation-tracking-and-management-action-plans]. Common practice assigns SLA targets by severity: roughly 15 to 30 days for critical findings, 30 to 60 days for high, 90 days for medium, and 180 days for low, with some federal guidance tighter at 15 days critical and 30 days high (w=0.172, weak backing) [https://blarrow.tech/ultimate-remediation-plan-documentation-guide/]. A launch gate can adopt these SLA bands or set its own; the gate-grade requirement is that every open Medium finding carries a target date and an owner, and that remediation efforts are validated rather than assumed (w=0.153, weak backing) [https://blog.falcony.io/en/exploring-security-audit-findings-prioritization-and-remediation-strategies].

Effective remediation strategy also treats audits as ongoing rather than one-time events, with recurring findings read as signals of systemic weakness (w=0.137, weak backing) [https://fedninjas.com/frequent-audit-findings-and-remediation-best-practices/]. For a gate inventory that means: the remediation plan for a v1 audit should feed the next audit cycle, not evaporate at launch.

## Handling sensitive findings: NDA and public summary

Audit findings are commercially and tactically sensitive, so the evidence set splits in two. Internally, the full report is protected; externally, a public summary carries the credibility. Responsible-disclosure practice points the same direction: findings are reported privately to the owner first, with a clear remediation window before any public disclosure (w=0.146, weak backing) [https://www.exposesecurity.com/], and disclosure notes should be documented as clear, auditable records that let security teams triage, verify, and remediate (w=0.191, weak backing) [https://roihacks.com/how-to-document-responsible-disclosure-notes/].

The gate-grade evidence pattern is therefore: an NDA or equivalent agreement covering the audit findings, a release authorization covering the findings' public summary, and a hash reference (such as SHA-256) from the public summary to the full report so the two are cryptographically tied without publishing the full text. The eCFR reporting regime illustrates why the split matters: formal regimes require statements about whether factors like deliberate disregard or negligence are evident (w=0.875, high backing) [https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-32/subtitle-A/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-117/section-117.8], statements an organization may owe publicly while the underlying detail stays protected.

## Freshness

An audit is a snapshot of the code and the pipeline at a point in time. Gate criteria should therefore date the report against the launch target and expire it: a report more than about 90 days stale at launch no longer attests to what is shipping, because the launch surface will have moved. The OSTIF framing of public confidence through the written report (w=0.811, high backing) [https://ostif.org/open-source-security-audit-minimum-standards-expectations/] only holds if the report describes the artifact that actually launches.

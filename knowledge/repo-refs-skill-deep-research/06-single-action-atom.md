# 06 - The Single-Action Atom: One Flip per Cycle by Geodesic Descent

Scope: the atomic unit of archive improvement: choosing the single missing primitive whose flip reduces geodesic distance to the ideal coverage pole the most, one flip per cycle.

## Why geodesic distance is the right metric

On a sphere the correct notion of distance between two points is not the chord but the arc. The great-circle distance, also called the orthodromic or spherical distance, is the distance between two points on a sphere measured along the great-circle arc between them (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great-circle_distance, jev weight 0.25, weak). The general concept is older still: in its original sense a geodesic was the shortest route between two points on the Earth's surface (https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic, jev weight 0.25, weak). For the lifted corpus, where each file sits on S2 and the ideal all-ones coverage vector sits at the pole, the distance from a file's point to that pole is the quantity the improvement step should minimize.

The formal grounding for computing it comes from the calculus of variations: using spherical coordinates one can define the great circle through two points and calculate the geodesic distance between them on the surface (https://web.mae.ufl.edu/uhk/GEODESIC-ON-SPHERE.pdf, jev weight 0.75, high). The machine-learning literature adds the reason geodesics beat ad hoc metrics: the appropriate geometric distance corresponds to the length of the shortest path along the manifold, which is the geodesic distance, and approximating it is challenging when the manifold itself is unknown (https://arxiv.org/html/1907.00296v2, jev weight 0.75, high). On S2 the manifold is known exactly, which is a benefit of the lift: the geodesic can be computed in closed form instead of approximated.

## Why one coordinate at a time

The "one flip per cycle" rule is greedy coordinate descent applied to a discrete space. Coordinate descent operates on a single variable at each iteration, improving the objective along one coordinate while holding all other variables fixed, and both theoretical and empirical evidence point to the efficiency of this approach for large-scale problems (https://proceedings.mlr.press/v108/fang20a/fang20a.pdf, jev weight 0.69, high). A mirror of the same paper repeats the definition: the algorithm improves the objective value along a single coordinate while holding all other variables fixed (https://friedlander.io/files/pdf/2020GreedMeetsSparsity.pdf, jev weight 0.73, high). The greedy selection rule itself has a formal basis: greedy coordinate descent selects the coordinate with the largest potential decrease, which is effective for sparse and high-dimensional problems, and the selection can be proven correct by induction at the first iteration (https://proceedings.mlr.press/v108/fang20a/fang20a-supp.pdf, jev weight 0.64, high).

Generalizations confirm that greedy selection scales beyond a single coordinate when needed: a published variant greedily selects two columns first, then obtains the new estimate by successive projection with orthogonalization onto the solution space the two selected hyperplanes define (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1877750323000893, jev weight 0.88, high). For an archive atom, that variant is the pattern to reach for if a single flip is insufficient, but the default remains one coordinate.

## Why greedy is acceptable on a discrete space

The alternative to greedy would be exhaustive search over all possible flips, and the optimization literature says why that is the wrong trade. Discrete optimization is a central problem in mathematical optimization, and binary optimization and sparse optimization are two common instances; these problems are NP-hard and difficult to solve in general, and combinatorial search methods such as branch-and-bound and exhaustive search find the global optimal solution but are costly (https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.06493, jev weight 0.80, high). A 9-bit binary space has only 9 candidate flips per cycle, so exhaustion is cheap per cycle; the greedy rule still wins because it composes across cycles without requiring a re-solve of the full space each time, and it inherits the coordinate-descent convergence behavior documented above (https://proceedings.mlr.press/v108/fang20a/fang20a.pdf, jev weight 0.69, high).

## The atom assembled

The single-action atom is therefore: from the current 9-bit coverage vector, enumerate the missing primitives (the zero bits); for each, compute the geodesic distance from the vector's lifted point to the ideal all-ones pole both before and after the hypothetical flip; select the flip with the largest reduction; apply exactly that one edit; and stop the cycle there. The geodesic metric is grounded in the spherical-distance literature (https://web.mae.ufl.edu/uhk/GEODESIC-ON-SPHERE.pdf, jev weight 0.75, high; https://arxiv.org/html/1907.00296v2, jev weight 0.75, high), the single-coordinate update is grounded in coordinate descent theory (https://proceedings.mlr.press/v108/fang20a/fang20a.pdf, jev weight 0.69, high), and the refusal to search the full flip space is grounded in the NP-hardness and cost of exhaustive discrete optimization (https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.06493, jev weight 0.80, high). Dictionary-grade definitions of distance carry no design weight and are recorded as context only (https://handwiki.org/wiki/Distance, jev weight 0.08, weak).

# Lamb modes of elastic spheres and Raman visibility

**Scope:** the free-vibration spectrum of an elastic sphere (spheroidal and torsional families), the labeling by angular momentum l and overtone n, the 2l+1 degeneracy, and the Raman selection-rule situation that decides which modes are visible in light scattering.

## Lamb's problem and the two families

The free elastic sphere's vibrations were first analyzed by Lamb in the Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society in 1881 ([CNRS, Vibrational eigenmodes of an isotropic sphere](https://saviot.cnrs.fr/lamb/index.en.html), jev weight 0.760). A modern note restates the setting: "Lamb proposed a theoretical aspect to investigate the spheroidal modes of a dry elastic sphere", and in spheroidal modes the volume or shape of the sphere changes ([ScienceDirect, A note on the spheroidal modes vibration of an elastic sphere in linear elasticity, 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0375960120304230), jev weight 0.895).

The modes come in two symmetry families. For the torsional modes the displacements are perpendicular to the plane of the figure; for the spheroidal modes the displacements are inside the plane (CNRS, weight 0.760). Each mode is labeled by the symmetry of the vibration (spheroidal or torsional), the angular momentum l, and the overtone index n, and the eigenfrequencies follow from the sphere's longitudinal and transverse sound velocities v_L and v_T through dimensionless reduced frequencies of the form omega R / v_L and omega R / v_T (CNRS, weight 0.760). Each (l, n) mode is 2l+1-fold degenerate, and the surface displacement pattern can be visualized in 3D for those degenerate components (CNRS, weight 0.760).

## Longitudinal versus transverse spheroidal modes

Saviot and Murray's analysis of the spheroidal modes of a free elastic sphere showed that "they can be qualitatively grouped into two categories: primarily longitudinal and primarily transverse", and that "analysis of inelastic light scattering intensity from confined acoustic phonons in nanoparticles requires an understanding of this qualitative distinction" ([arXiv:cond-mat/0506353, Longitudinal versus transverse spheroidal vibrational modes of an elastic sphere; published as Physical Review B 72, 205433 (2005)](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0506353), jev weight 0.856). The same paper explicitly corrects common misconceptions about spheroidal modes (arXiv:cond-mat/0506353, weight 0.856).

Within the spheroidal family the Raman-relevant modes are specific and not uniform. The (SPH, l = 2, n) modes "are of primary interest when trying to understand Raman intensities"; two separate mechanisms couple the l = 2 acoustic vibrations to the surface plasmon resonance and lead to Raman scattering; earlier work predicted that l = 2 modes with differing overtone n contribute to the Raman spectrum in a highly non-uniform way; and the basic nature of the l = 0 modes is "much more clear because of their simplicity and symmetry" ([arXiv:cond-mat/0506353 full text](https://arxiv.org/html/cond-mat/0506353v3), jev weight 0.856). For the l = 0 modes the relevant surface quantity reduces to the radial uniform component by symmetry (arXiv:cond-mat/0506353, weight 0.856).

## Selection rules: agreed existence, contested form

The elastic vibrations of spherical nanoparticles "have been widely studied by Raman scattering. However, there exist more than one set of selection rules for Raman scattering" from those eigenmodes ([Chemical Physics Letters, 2007, Selection rules for Raman scattering from eigenmodes of spherical nanoparticles](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0009261407004812), jev weight 0.915). That is, the community agrees that a selection rule filters which (l, n) modes appear in a Raman spectrum, but the exact form of the rule has historically been stated in more than one way. Recent work extracts the Raman selection rules of spherical nanoparticles embedded in a dielectric medium and treats elastic anisotropy effects in the Raman energy density framework ([Physical Review B 106, 165425 (2022), Raman energy density in the context of acoustoplasmonics](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevB.106.165425), jev weight 0.781).

## The visibility filter, structurally

The structural content that survives across all of these sources:

1. The free elastic sphere has a discrete spectrum organized by two families (spheroidal, torsional), an angular momentum l, and an overtone n (CNRS, weight 0.760).
2. Each (l, n) level carries a 2l+1 degeneracy from rotational symmetry (CNRS, weight 0.760).
3. Light scattering does not see all of the spectrum: a selection rule suppresses most modes, and the low-l spheroidal modes (l = 0 breathing and l = 2 quadrupolar) are the ones with clean, well-understood Raman visibility, while higher modes contribute non-uniformly (arXiv:cond-mat/0506353, weight 0.856; Chemical Physics Letters 2007, weight 0.915).
4. The qualitative character of a spheroidal mode (primarily longitudinal versus primarily transverse) matters for the scattering intensity and was itself a source of misconceptions in the literature (arXiv:cond-mat/0506353, weight 0.856).

The lesson for any framework importing this structure: the sphere's rotational symmetry gives the level scheme and its degeneracies, and the same symmetry's selection rules decide observability. The filter is not an extra assumption; it is the rotational structure read twice, once for energies and once for couplings.

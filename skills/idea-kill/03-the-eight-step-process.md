# 03 - The eight-step process

Scope: the full pipeline from loading the idea to naming resurrection triggers, step by step, with the external techniques each step borrows.

## Steps 1 and 2: load the idea, name the bet

Step 1 accepts any of four artifact classes: raw idea text, a one-pager (from idea-refine, ideate-solo, or the workspace), a spec (from spec-driven-development), or a plan or design doc. The reviewer reads it fully and notes the claim being made and the bet being placed (source doc).

Step 2 distills the artifact into a single sentence: the bet, the claim that must be true for the idea to work. If the bet cannot be named in one sentence, the idea is unfocused, and the source doc makes that itself a verdict: PAUSE with the reason "can't name the bet" (source doc). The bet sentence is the hinge of the whole review; every later step tests it.

## Step 3: steelman the opposition

Step 3 requires the strongest possible critique, not the easiest one, covering at least three of five angles: why this won't work (the failure mechanism), why this is unnecessary (what already solves this, what was tried and failed), why this is un-scaleable (what breaks at 10x or 100x users), why this is un-defensible (what stops a well-funded competitor from copying it in 6 months), and why this is a vitamin rather than a painkiller (who would pay, and how often they would feel the pain) (source doc).

The steelman technique has a standard definition: engaging with the strongest, most reasonable form of the opposing argument rather than a weakened straw man (https://www.merriam-webster.com/slang/steelman, weight 0.56). The skill's requirement to cover at least three of the five named angles is what makes the steelman operational instead of decorative: a reviewer cannot satisfy it with a single generic objection.

## Step 4: surface second-order effects

Step 4 asks what happens if the idea works as intended, in both directions: positive cascades (new opportunities success unlocks) and negative cascades (new problems success creates, with the source doc listing adoption friction, lock-in, support burden, security surface, organizational complexity, and regulatory exposure) (source doc).

The underlying discipline is second-order thinking: tracing the consequences of the consequences rather than stopping at the first effect (https://zenofthinking.com/en/models/second-order-thinking, weight 0.25, weak backing). Practitioner treatments make the same point in decision-type terms: a decision's second-order landscape depends on how reversible it is, which is why tools that classify decisions by reversibility pair naturally with cascade analysis (https://www.theuncertaintyproject.org/tools/decision-types, weight 0.31, weak backing). The net sign of the cascades feeds the verdict directly: net-negative cascades support KILL; net-positive cascades support SHIP.

## Steps 5 and 6: the un-testable bet and the verdict

Step 5 returns to the bet from Step 2 and asks whether it can be tested cheaply. An un-testable bet is a kill signal even for an otherwise strong idea, because the project cannot learn cheaply whether to keep going (source doc). This step is covered in depth in doc 05.

Step 6 produces exactly one of KILL, PAUSE, REVISE, SHIP, with the semantics documented in doc 04. The source doc prohibits "maybe" and "interesting but..."; the verdict is one word (source doc).

## Steps 7 and 8: reasons and resurrection triggers

Step 7 lists 3 to 5 specific reasons justifying the verdict. Each reason must cite a concrete observation from Steps 3 through 5, not a vibe. If the reviewer's only reason is a feeling, the structural reason behind the feeling must be extracted and used instead (source doc).

Step 8 names resurrection triggers: what would have to change to make the idea viable again. The source doc's examples: a competitor shutting down their free tier, a regulatory change flipping a second-order effect, an acquired capability making the un-testable bet testable. Triggers make the kill verdict reusable rather than terminal (source doc).

## The pipeline as a unit

Read in order, the eight steps convert an artifact into a verdict through a fixed set of questions: what is the bet, what is the strongest case against it, what happens if it works, can the bet be tested cheaply. The output document (doc 06) mirrors the pipeline one-to-one, which is what makes the verdict auditable: every field of the output corresponds to one step's evidence.

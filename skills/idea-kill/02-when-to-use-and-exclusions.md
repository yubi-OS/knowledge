# 02 - When to use and when not to use

Scope: the entry points for a kill verdict (raw idea, one-pager, spec, plan), the explicit exclusions, and how the practiced premortem and kill-criteria traditions relate to the skill.

## Entry points (source doc)

The source doc lists the inputs the skill accepts: a raw idea of one or more sentences, a one-pager produced by idea-refine or ideate-solo or found in the workspace, a spec produced by spec-driven-development, or a plan or design doc. The process in every case is the same: read the artifact fully, note the claim being made and the bet being placed (source doc).

The trigger phrases are explicit: "kill this idea", "is this worth it?", "should I drop this?". The skill also fires when an existing plan or one-pager feels weak and the requester wants a verdict rather than more encouragement (source doc).

## The exclusions (source doc)

Three cases are named as wrong-tool cases. First, a freshly minted idea with no prior review should go to idea-refine or ideate-solo first, because the kill verdict needs something concrete to act on. Second, a user who is emotionally invested and explicitly wants encouragement should not get a kill-pulse: idea-kill surfaces reasons to kill, which reads as an attack when the requester wanted validation. Third, a politically charged verdict the agent cannot honestly produce should route to negative-skill-space, which maps gaps without making a recommendation (source doc).

The common thread is honesty capacity: the skill produces an honest verdict only when the requester actually wants one and the artifact is concrete enough to judge.

## The premortem as practiced analogue

The closest external practice to a kill-pulse is Gary Klein's premortem. The Harvard Business Review article that popularized it describes the technique: before a project starts, the team assumes the project has already failed and generates the reasons why. Prospective hindsight, imagining that an event has already occurred, increases the ability to correctly identify reasons for future outcomes, and the premortem converts that into a group exercise where skepticism is legitimate and dissent is expected rather than punished (https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem, weight 0.85). Klein's own description of the method matches: the premortem legitimizes doubt by making it the assigned job of every participant (https://www.gary-klein.com/premortem, weight 0.83).

The Wikipedia entry on pre-mortems describes the same structure at a survey level and notes its use as a risk-reduction step at project kickoff (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-mortem, weight 0.47, weak backing). The relation to idea-kill is direct: a premortem is the steelmanning step of the skill run as a group ritual before commitment; idea-kill runs it as a verdict.

## Kill criteria and stage gates

Product and project management practice also carries the kill concept forward. Kill criteria are pre-committed conditions under which a project is stopped; advocates argue that naming them in advance removes the decision from the moment of highest sunk-cost pressure (https://www.mindtheproduct.com/the-case-for-kill-criteria-in-product-management/, weight 0.39, weak backing). The stage-gate tradition formalizes this as gates between phases where each gate can end the project, not just continue it (https://asana.com/resources/stage-gate-process, weight 0.29, weak backing).

Where the dig world has moved past the source doc, the drift is modest: the source doc treats the kill verdict as an event-driven review of a single artifact, while the practiced tradition (premortem, stage-gate, kill criteria) embeds the same judgment as scheduled checkpoints. The dated correction is that practitioners increasingly pre-commit the criteria; idea-kill supplies the criteria implicitly through its fixed review structure (bet, steelman, second-order effects, testability) rather than asking the team to define gates in advance.

## When the verdict is wanted, not the review

One boundary from the source doc deserves emphasis: the skill is for a verdict, not for encouragement. If the requester wants validation, the skill is the wrong tool by its own definition (source doc). This is also why the exclusions route work to upstream skills (idea-refine, ideate-solo) or alternatives (negative-skill-space) instead of degrading the review into something agreeable.

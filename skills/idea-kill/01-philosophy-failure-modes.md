# 01 - Philosophy and failure modes

Scope: why most ideas should die, the two failure modes (polite-yes and sunk-cost) that bias continuation, and why the verdict itself is the deliverable.

## The core philosophy (source doc)

The source doc (yubi-OS/yubiOS skills/idea-kill/SKILL.md) states the position plainly: most ideas should die, and many do not die because there is no cheap, honest way to evaluate them. Two named failure modes do the damage. The polite-yes failure mode is the case where the reviewer agrees with a weak direction to be agreeable. The sunk-cost failure mode is the case where the reviewer has already invested in the idea and wants it to be true. Both bias the review toward continuation. The skill exists to force a structured verdict, one of KILL, PAUSE, REVISE, or SHIP, with explicit reasoning. The verdict is the deliverable, and hedging is not allowed (source doc).

## Polite-yes: agreeableness as a structural bias

The agreeable-reviewer problem is not a personality quirk; it is a documented behavioral pattern. The Big Five trait of agreeableness measures the tendency toward cooperation and social accommodation, and highly agreeable people tend to avoid conflict and maintain harmony even at the cost of accuracy (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agreeableness, weight 0.66).

The narrower form of the same problem is acquiescence bias: the tendency of a respondent to agree with a statement regardless of its content, simply because agreement is the socially cheaper answer (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acquiescence_bias, weight 0.74). In an idea review, the equivalent of the acquiescent respondent is the reviewer who nods along to the idea author's framing. The source doc's polite-yes failure mode is this bias applied to idea evaluation: the cost of disagreement is social, so weak ideas survive.

## Sunk-cost: the invested reviewer

The sunk cost effect is one of the most replicated findings in behavioral decision research. Rational choice theory says only future costs and benefits should influence a decision; observed behavior repeatedly deviates, with people continuing commitments because of resources already spent (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunk_cost, weight 0.62). Economic and psychological studies of the effect, including the classic escalation-of-commitment paradigm, find the effect is robust across decision domains and is stronger when the decision maker feels personal responsibility for the initial investment (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167268121001293, weight 0.81).

Research management shows the same shape at the level of projects: a commentary on sunk-cost bias argues that researchers persist in failing research projects well past the point where termination is justified, precisely because the resources already spent make stopping feel like waste (https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/anie.202208429, weight 0.77). This is the exact mechanism the source doc names as the sunk-cost failure mode: the reviewer who already invested wants the idea to be true, so the verdict drifts toward continuation regardless of the evidence.

## Why the verdict is the deliverable

The source doc's answer to both biases is structural rather than motivational. It does not ask the reviewer to be braver or more objective; it forces a fixed output: exactly one of four one-word verdicts, backed by 3 to 5 concrete reasons, with resurrection triggers attached even to a KILL. Hedging ("interesting but...") is prohibited by the output format itself, not by exhortation (source doc). The design insight is that the failure modes above operate through vagueness: an unstructured review can always end in a shrug. A structured verdict removes the shrug.

## What the external literature adds

The external dig supports both halves of the philosophy. The sunk-cost literature (the ScienceDirect effect study at weight 0.81 and the Wiley project-termination commentary at weight 0.77) supplies the mechanism for why invested reviewers cannot be trusted to self-correct. The agreeableness and acquiescence literature (weights 0.66 and 0.74) supplies the mechanism for why socially motivated reviewers drift to yes. Neither mechanism requires the reviewer to be dishonest; both operate silently, which is why the source doc insists the skill runs as a forced one-word output rather than a discussion.
